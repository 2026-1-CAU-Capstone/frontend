## Song Info

- **Title:** When I Fall In Love
- **Composer:** Victor Young / Edward Heyman (1952, from *One Minute to Zero*)
- **Center Key:** E♭ Major (jam-session key; also G, C, D♭)
- **Form:** 32 bars ABAC (each section 8 bars); ballad
- **Source:** The Jazz Pursuit — "When I Fall In Love (V. Young) Tutorial & Analysis [Part 1]" (https://youtu.be/s6PoKMuWxqU) & "Reharms and Extra Chords [Part 2]" (https://youtu.be/HGtu1AoMvaw)

### Diatonic Chords (E♭ Major)

EbMaj7 (I), Fm7 (ii), Gm7 (iii), AbMaj7 (IV), Bb7 (V), Cm7 (vi), Dm7♭5 (vii°)

### Chord Progression

**A section (foundation):**
| EbMaj7 | EbMaj7 | EbMaj7 | EbMaj7 | EbMaj7 AbMaj7 | Fm7 | F7 | Bb7 |

**B section (foundation):**
| EbMaj7 | AbMaj7 | Gm7 | Cm7 | Fm7 C7 | Fm7 | Fm7 Bb7 | EbMaj7 |

**C section (foundation):**
| EbMaj7 | AbMaj7 | Fm7 (C7→Fm7) | Fm7 Bb7 EbMaj7 | EbMaj7 | Fm7 Bb7 EbMaj7 |

## Level 1: General Concepts

### 1-1. The secondary dominant in the A section

**instruction:** Why does the ii chord (Fm7) become F7 in the A section?

**response:** It's a secondary dominant. The A section moves to chord ii (Fm7), then turns it into a dominant — F7 — built on the 2nd degree. A secondary dominant is the "second dominant of the key": the primary dominant is the V (Bb7, which resolves to I), and F7 is the V *of* that V. So instead of resolving to I, F7 forms a V-I into Bb7 (the primary dominant), which then carries home to EbMaj7. It's a chain: secondary dominant → primary dominant → tonic.

### 1-2. The B section as a long 1-4-3-6-2-5

**instruction:** What is the underlying shape of the B section?

**response:** Effectively a long 1-4-3-6-2-5 in E♭: EbMaj7 (I) → AbMaj7 (IV) → Gm7 (iii) → Cm7 (vi) → Fm7 (ii). When it lands on Fm7 (ii) you'd normally expect a ii-V-I home, but it's too early (four bars left), so it re-cadences to buy time — a V-I back to Fm7 (C7 → Fm7) — before the real ii-V-I (Fm7 – Bb7 – EbMaj7). Almost every chord here is diatonic to E♭ (only the secondary dominant in the A leaves the key), a great example of creating interest while staying inside one key center.

### 1-3. A melody strong enough to carry heavy reharmonization

**instruction:** Why can When I Fall In Love take so many reharmonizations?

**response:** Because the melody is so strong and uses only the E♭ major scale, the harmony underneath can be tweaked and embellished extensively without clashing. The A section opens with a phrase — rising in fourths (B♭ → E♭ → A♭) then falling 3rd to root — that is then repeated and developed, and the B and C sections reuse that motif transposed. A diatonic, motif-driven melody like this is a blank canvas for reharm, which is exactly why the tune is a reharmonization favorite.

## Level 2: Song-Specific Analysis

### 2-1. The A and B sections in full

**instruction:** Analyze the foundation harmony of the A and B sections.

**response:** The A section sits on chord I (EbMaj7) for four-plus bars, moves to IV (AbMaj7), then ii (Fm7) which becomes the secondary dominant F7 → V (Bb7) → I. The B section is a long 1-4-3-6-2-5 (EbMaj7 – AbMaj7 – Gm7 – Cm7 – Fm7), re-cadences to Fm7 (C7 → Fm7) to delay the resolution, then a ii-V-I (Fm7 – Bb7 – EbMaj7) lands at the top of the next A. The motivic melody develops by repetition: the A's rising-fourths phrase is restated and extended, and the B transposes it up a step to outline Fm7.

### 2-2. The C section and the melodic peak

**instruction:** How does the C section differ and where is the climax?

**response:** The C section opens like the B (EbMaj7 → AbMaj7), then uses a V-I to chord ii (C7 → Fm7) and a quick ii-V-I home (Fm7 – Bb7 – EbMaj7), repeating the ii-V-I in the last line. The melodic peak lands here: a leap to the ♯11 of AbMaj7 (D) — a tweaked suspension that falls to the 3rd (C). The tune then closes with a classic device, restating the opening phrase of the whole piece (the rising-fourths motif) to round off the form.

## Level 3: Performance Decisions

### 3-1. Filling the static A section (1-6-2-5)

**instruction:** The A section sits on E♭ for four bars — how do I keep it moving?

**response:** Drop in two 1-6-2-5 progressions to animate the static tonic: EbMaj7 (I) → Cm7 (vi) → Fm7 (ii) → Bb7 (V) → back to EbMaj7, twice. This keeps the bass and chords moving while still circling home. Add the same 1-6-2-5 to the last line of the C section (insert Cm7 in the fifth bar) where the opening phrase returns. In bar 6, you can also set up the F7 with its own V-I — C7 → F7 — and since the melody note is A♭, spell it C7♭13.

### 3-2. Reharmonizing the B section with minor ii-Vs and a 1-4-3-6

**instruction:** How can I add tension and release to the B section?

**response:** Two moves. (1) In bars 3–4, where Gm7 → Cm7 → Fm7 are all the same chord type (no tension), insert a 1-4-3-6 (EbMaj7 – AbMaj7 – Gm7 – Cm7) to propel toward the Fm7, and make the Gm7–Cm7 into a minor ii-V (Gm7♭5 – C7♭9) since the target Fm7 is minor. (2) In bar 6, embellish the re-cadence's V-I into a minor ii-V-i (Gm7♭5 – C7♭9 – Fm7). The same minor ii-V (Gm7♭5 – C7♭9) also enriches the C section's bar 3–4 cadence to Fm7.

### 3-3. Three favorite reharms (tritone sub, backdoor, set-up dominant)

**instruction:** What are some tasteful reharmonizations to use in my own version?

**response:** (1) **Tritone sub:** in bar 1 of the B section, set up the AbMaj7 with its V (Eb7), then substitute the tritone — A7♯11 (the melody E♭ is the ♯11) — for a chromatic, hipper approach (the same fits bar 1 of the C). (2) **Backdoor ii-V:** in bar 4 of the C section, replace the regular ii-V to E♭ with Abm7 – Db7, where Db7 resolves up a whole step to EbMaj7; Nat King Cole drops these everywhere — they can substitute almost any ii-V as long as they fit the melody (also works in the last bar of the B). (3) **Set-up dominant:** in bar 5 of the B, precede the Gm7♭5 with its V (D7); since the melody note F is the ♯9 of D7, make it D7 altered. Above all, the melody is king — these tricks should support it, and they suit solo piano, voice duo, or your own improvising rather than every chorus.
