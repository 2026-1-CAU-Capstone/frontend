## Song Info

- **Title:** Bye Bye Blackbird
- **Composer:** Ray Henderson / Mort Dixon (1926)
- **Center Key:** F Major
- **Form:** 32 bars, A B C A1 (each section 8 bars; A1 is a variation on A)
- **Source:** The Jazz Pursuit — "Bye Bye Blackbird Tutorial & Analysis" (https://youtu.be/BiE4IUCS8yI)

### Diatonic Chords (F Major)

FMaj7 (I), Gm7 (ii), Am7 (iii), BbMaj7 (IV), C7 (V), Dm7 (vi), Em7♭5 (vii°)

### Chord Progression

**A section:**
| FMaj7 | Gm7 C7 FMaj7 | FMaj7 | Gm7 C7 FMaj7 | FMaj7 | Dm7 (D7) | Gm7 | C7 |

**B section (centered on chord ii):**
| Gm7 | D7 Gm7 | C7 Gm7 | Gm7 C7 | FMaj7 | ... |

**C section (descending dominants):**
| F7 | E7 | Eb7 | D7 | Gm7 | Gm7 | Db7 | C7 |

## Level 1: General Concepts

### 1-1. Re-cadencing on chord I

**instruction:** How does the A section keep moving while sitting on the tonic?

**response:** Through re-cadencing — cadencing back onto the chord you're already on. The A section rests on chord I (FMaj7), then re-cadences with a ii-V-I (Gm7 – C7 – FMaj7) that resolves right back to FMaj7. It does this twice, keeping the harmony and bass active even though the section is essentially static on the tonic. Re-cadencing is the classic trick for animating a held chord.

### 1-2. The 1-6-2-5 with a dominant vi

**instruction:** What progression closes the A section, and why turn vi into a dominant?

**response:** The A section ends with a 1-6-2-5: FMaj7 (I) → Dm7 (vi) → Gm7 (ii) → C7 (V). The vi chord is diatonically Dm7, but tweaking it to D7 (a dominant) creates a V-I pull into the next chord, Gm7. That secondary-dominant move adds momentum and is one of the most common reharmonizations of any 1-6-2-5 turnaround.

### 1-3. A chain of descending dominants

**instruction:** What's happening in the first half of the C section?

**response:** The C section is the tune's biggest harmonic event: four dominant chords, each lasting a bar and falling by a half step — F7 → E7 → E♭7 → D7. Even chord I appears as F7 (dominant, not major) to join the chain. The last of them, D7, becomes the V of a V-I cadence into Gm7 (chord ii). A chromatic line of dominants like this generates strong forward drive and a real sense of harmonic adventure.

## Level 2: Song-Specific Analysis

### 2-1. The A and B sections

**instruction:** How do the A and B sections relate?

**response:** The A section is built around chord I (FMaj7), re-cadenced twice with ii-V-I, then closed with a 1-6-2-5 (vi made dominant). The B section shifts its center to chord ii (Gm7) and continues the re-cadence theme: a V-I re-cadence (D7 → Gm7), then a softer IV-I re-cadence (C7 → Gm7), before a ii-V-I (Gm7 – C7 – FMaj7) resolves home. So A orbits the tonic and B orbits the ii chord, each kept alive by re-cadencing.

### 2-2. The C section's tritone approach and A1 ending

**instruction:** How do the C section and A1 wrap up the form?

**response:** After the descending dominants resolve to Gm7 (held two bars), the C section heads toward the A1 with a ii-V-I — but with a sting: a half-step approach to the V, D♭7 → C7 → FMaj7. The A1 then mirrors A but must *end* the tune rather than set up the bridge: it re-cadences (Gm7 – C7) without reaching I, then runs a 3-6-2-5-1 (Am7 – D7 – Gm7 – C7 – F). Turning the 3 and 6 into a minor ii-V (Am7♭5 – D7) adds extra momentum to the final cadence.

## Level 3: Performance Decisions

### 3-1. Spelling the descending dominants in the C section

**instruction:** How do I solo over F7 – E7 – E♭7 – D7?

**response:** Treat each as its own dominant and connect them chromatically — the melody itself walks down the F major scale and chromatically resolves into each new chord's 3rd (e.g. landing on F♯, the 3rd of D7, the first non-key note of the tune). Target the 3rd of each dominant on the downbeat and let the half-step root motion guide your line. Mixolydian on each, or a single chromatic descending idea shifted bar to bar, both work well.

### 3-2. The B-section bass reharmonization

**instruction:** Is there a smoother way to voice the start of the B section?

**response:** Yes — read the first four chords as a descending G-minor line cliché. Instead of Gm7 – D7 – Gm7 – C7, play Gm7 → GmMaj7 → Gm7 → Gm6, so the bass falls chromatically G → F♯ → F → E. Same harmonic function, but the inner/bass voice descends by half steps, giving the B section a smoother, more sophisticated pull. It's a direct substitute for the re-cadence chords.

### 3-3. Common extra chords

**instruction:** What tasteful additions can I drop into the form?

**response:** Three idiomatic ones: (1) in bar 6 of the A, swap the D7 for A♭°7 — the melody note G becomes the major 7th, so use a diminished-major-7 voicing; (2) the G-minor line-cliché bass move in the B section (above); and (3) in the last two bars of the B section, where it sits on FMaj7, drop in a 3-6-2-5 (Am7 – D7 – Gm7 – C7) to keep the harmony moving and propel into the C section. Use them sparingly for color rather than every chorus.
