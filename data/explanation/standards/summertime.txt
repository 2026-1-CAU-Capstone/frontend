## Song Info

- **Title:** Summertime
- **Composer:** George Gershwin (from *Porgy and Bess*, 1935)
- **Center Key:** A minor
- **Form:** 16 bars (usually repeated for two verses)
- **Source:** The Jazz Pursuit — "Summertime (George Gershwin) Tutorial & Analysis" (https://youtu.be/HS_mtZOrnDQ)

### Diatonic Chords (A minor / relative major C)

Am (i), Bm7♭5 (ii°), CMaj7 (♭III / relative major), Dm (iv), Em (v), FMaj7 (♭VI), G7 (♭VII)

### Chord Progression

**Full form (16 bars):**
| Am | Am | Am | Am | Dm | Dm | Em | Em |
| Am | Am | Am | Am | C | C | Am | Am |

---

## Level 1: General Concepts

### 1-1. Natural minor (modal) movement vs. functional minor

**instruction:** Summertime stays on minor chords like Am, Dm, Em without strong dominant cadences. How should I think about that?

**response:** Summertime is built almost entirely from the A natural minor scale (the same notes as its relative major, C). The motion is i (Am) to iv (Dm) to v (Em) — and crucially the v chord is a plain Em (minor), not an E7 dominant. Because there is no leading tone (no G♯) and no dominant 7th pulling to the tonic, the harmony feels modal and folk-like rather than functionally cadential. The whole tune can be understood as coloring within A natural minor, which is why a single scale carries you through almost the entire form.

### 1-2. Borrowing the relative major for brightness

**instruction:** Why does a dark minor tune like Summertime suddenly go to a C major chord near the end?

**response:** C major is the relative major of A minor (it can also be labeled chord ♭III). Because A minor and C major share the exact same notes, sliding to the C chord introduces a brief shaft of light without leaving the key. In Summertime this happens near the end of the form (two bars of C) before returning home to Am, giving a momentary lift before the tune settles back into its minor mood. It is the simplest way to brighten a minor tune while staying entirely diatonic.

## Level 2: Song-Specific Analysis

### 2-1. The whole 16-bar form

**instruction:** Walk me through the harmony of Summertime.

**response:** The form is unusually simple: four bars of chord i (Am), then two bars of chord iv (Dm), then two bars of chord v (Em). That is the first half. The second half returns to chord i (Am) for four bars, moves to the relative major / chord ♭III (C) for two bars, and finishes back on chord i (Am) for the last two bars. So the entire harmonic story is i → iv → v → i → ♭III → i, all drawn from A natural minor. Its brevity (16 bars, often repeated for the two lyric verses) makes it a favorite first minor-key vehicle.

### 2-2. Why the v chord stays minor

**instruction:** Could I play E7 instead of Em on the v chord?

**response:** You can — and many players do raise it to E7 (or E7♭9) to create a stronger pull back to Am via the leading tone G♯. But the original, written sound is a plain Em, which keeps everything inside A natural minor and preserves the modal, unhurried character of the tune. The choice is expressive: keep Em for the folk-like original color, or substitute E7 when you want a more functional, bluesy dominant tension resolving home. Knowing it is "naturally" minor tells you the default scale is A natural minor over that chord.

## Level 3: Performance Decisions

### 3-1. One scale for almost the whole tune

**instruction:** What is the simplest scale approach to soloing on Summertime?

**response:** Because the changes are nearly all diatonic to A natural minor, you can solo through most of the form with the A natural minor scale (equivalently, think C major from A). The A minor pentatonic (A–C–D–E–G) is an even safer subset that fits i, iv, and the relative-major chord beautifully and gives the tune its bluesy, vocal quality. Save scale changes only for the moments you choose to alter — for example if you raise the v chord to E7, switch to A harmonic minor (or E altered) for those two bars to spell the leading tone.

### 3-2. Phrasing a 16-bar minor blues-adjacent form

**instruction:** How do I keep a short 16-bar minor tune interesting across choruses?

**response:** With only i, iv, v, and the relative major to work with, contrast comes from phrasing and register rather than from chasing changes. Lean into the bluesy minor-pentatonic vocabulary on the long Am sections, mark the move to Dm (iv) and Em (v) by targeting their chord tones, and use the brief C-major lift as a "release" moment — brighter, higher, more lyrical — before returning to the minor home. Because the form is so short, develop one motive across several choruses instead of trying to say everything at once.
