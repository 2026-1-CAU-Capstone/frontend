## Song Info

- **Title:** Hymn To Freedom
- **Composer:** Oscar Peterson (1963, from *Night Train*; lyrics later by Harriette Hamilton)
- **Center Key:** B♭ Major
- **Form:** 32 bars AABA (each section 8 bars)
- **Source:** The Jazz Pursuit — "Hymn To Freedom (O. Peterson) Analysis & Tutorial" (https://youtu.be/IdoaO6z0QGI)

### Diatonic Chords (B♭ Major)

BbMaj7 (I), Cm7 (ii), Dm7 (iii), EbMaj7 (IV), F7 (V), Gm7 (vi), Am7♭5 (vii°)

### Chord Progression

**A section (mostly triads, hymn style):**
| Bb | D7 Gm | Bb7 Eb | E°7 Bb/F Bb | Eb | E°7 Bb/F Gm | Cm Dm... | C7 F7 Bb |

(I → V-I to vi → V-I to IV → plagal cadence (IV–I) disguised with passing E°7 → development to vi → ii-V-I home, with the ii made a secondary dominant.)

**B section:**
| F | F | Bb | D7 Gm | Bb7 Eb | E°7 Bb/F Gm | C7 F7 | Bb |

## Level 1: General Concepts

### 1-1. A jazz tune built like a church hymn

**instruction:** Why does Hymn To Freedom use plain triads instead of jazz 7th chords?

**response:** As its title and origin suggest, Hymn To Freedom is rooted in church music — Oscar Peterson's nod to the civil rights movement — so it's voiced like a hymn: most chords are simple triads with only a few specific 7th chords. Notably, even the V chords in its V-I cadences are major triads, not dominant 7ths (e.g. D major, not D7, resolving to Gm). The 7th chords appear only at key functional moments, which is what gives the piece its hymnal, gospel character rather than a standard jazz sound.

### 1-2. The plagal cadence (IV–I) and how Peterson disguises it

**instruction:** What is the plagal cadence in this tune, and how is it dressed up?

**response:** After establishing I, vi, and IV with perfect (V-I) cadences, Peterson breaks the pattern with a plagal cadence — chord IV moving to chord I (EbMaj → Bb), the "amen" cadence of church music. He disguises it two ways: (1) he delays the resolution by landing first on Bb/F (the tonic over its 5th) before the root-position Bb on beat 3, and (2) he inserts a passing E°7 between the E♭ and the Bb/F, so the bass rises chromatically E♭ → E → F. The plagal cadence is the harmonic signature of the hymn style.

### 1-3. The same chord, different function

**instruction:** Why is the B♭ chord sometimes a triad and sometimes B♭7?

**response:** It depends on what the chord is doing. When B♭ acts as chord I (the tonic), it stays a plain major triad. But when B♭ functions as the V of a V-I cadence into chord IV (E♭), it's tweaked to a dominant 7 — Bb7 — to create that dominant pull. Same root, different role: tonic = triad, secondary dominant = dominant 7. Reading the function tells you the quality.

## Level 2: Song-Specific Analysis

### 2-1. The A section in full

**instruction:** Analyze the Hymn To Freedom A section.

**response:** It opens on Bb (I), then a V-I to the relative minor vi (D → Gm), then a V-I to IV (Bb7 → Eb). Having touched I, IV, and vi, Peterson breaks the run with a disguised plagal cadence: Eb → E°7 → Bb/F → Bb. He then develops that cadence (Eb → E°7 → Bb/F) but resolves instead to vi via a quick V-I (D → Gm). The last two bars slow the harmonic rhythm and run a ii-V-I home, but with the ii made a secondary dominant: C7 → F7 → Bb (a chain of V-I cadences).

### 2-2. The bridge: new material that paraphrases the A

**instruction:** How is the B section different from a typical jazz bridge?

**response:** Unlike a contrasting jazz bridge, Hymn To Freedom's B section opens with just two bars of genuinely new harmony — chord V (F major), heard only in passing before — then *paraphrases* the A section. It falls to Bb, runs V-I to vi (D → Gm), V-I to IV (Bb7 → Eb), the disguised plagal cadence (Eb → E°7 → Bb/F) resolving to Gm, and the same secondary-dominant ii-V-I home (C7 → F7 → Bb). So the bridge reinforces the form by sitting on the previously-unheard V chord, then leans back on familiar A-section harmony.

### 2-3. The melody: simple and diatonic

**instruction:** What's the character of the melody?

**response:** In true hymn style, the melody uses only the B♭ major scale and is deliberately simple, letting the harmony do the talking. The A-section phrases mostly walk down the major scale (3rd to root) or fall through the tonic triad (5th–3rd–root), and the closing phrase just outlines the root notes of the dominant chords. This plainness is the point — the emotional weight comes from the gospel harmony and voicings underneath, not from melodic complexity.

## Level 3: Performance Decisions

### 3-1. Gospel voicings: root on the outside, inner voices walking

**instruction:** What is the signature Oscar Peterson voicing in this tune?

**response:** On the dominant chords (e.g. C7, F7 in the first-time ending), Peterson puts the root at both the top and bottom of the voicing, with the 3rd and 5th on the inside doing all the work. The inner voices (5th in the left thumb, 3rd in the right thumb) walk up through the chord — 5th/3rd → 6th/4th → 7th/5th — and back down, while the outer roots stay put. This walking-inner-voice, fixed-root shape is a classic gospel voicing straight from the church music that inspired the piece.

### 3-2. Reharmonizing via inversions (bass movement)

**instruction:** How did Peterson and Ray Brown vary the bass line in the A section?

**response:** In a hymn, the equivalent of jazz substitutions is using inversions to create more interesting bass motion. Three options for the A section: (1) in bars 5–6, voice D as D/F♯ so the bass rises chromatically E♭ → E°7 → F (Bb/F) → F♯ → G (Gm); (2) in bars 1–2, voice the V chords in first inversion (D/F♯ → Gm, Bb7/D → Eb) for a chromatic approach to each target; (3) Ray Brown's favorite — a descending bass: Bb → D/A → Gm → F♯m → Fm → Bb7 → Eb, turning the move to IV into a ii-V-I (Fm – Bb7 – Eb) with a chromatic G m → F♯m → Fm descent. Because the harmony is so consistent, you can experiment with inversions all over the tune.

### 3-3. Melody harmonization in the bridge

**instruction:** How does Peterson harmonize the opening bridge melody?

**response:** Over the two bars of chord V (F major), the melody rocks between the 5th (C) and 6th (D) of F. Peterson mirrors that in the harmony: when the melody rises to D he changes the chord to Gm, and when it falls back to C he returns to F major. F major and Gm are chords I and ii in F, so rocking between them over a held F bass creates gentle tension and release that matches the melody — a simple, vocal, hymn-like harmonization.
