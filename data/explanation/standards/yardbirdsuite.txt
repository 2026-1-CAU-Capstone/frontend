## Song Info

- **Title:** Yardbird Suite
- **Composer:** Charlie Parker (1946, Dial session)
- **Center Key:** C Major
- **Form:** 32 bars AABA (each section 8 bars)
- **Source:** The Jazz Pursuit — "Yardbird Suite (C. Parker) Analysis & Tutorial" (https://youtu.be/VOKztg4GXHM)

### Diatonic Chords (C Major)

CMaj7 (I), Dm7 (ii), Em7 (iii), FMaj7 (IV), G7 (V), Am7 (vi), Bm7♭5 (vii°)

### Chord Progression

**A section (8 bars):**
| C6 | Fm7 Bb7 | C6 (Bb7) A7 | D7 | G7 | C6 Am7 | Dm7 G7 | C6 |

**B section (8 bars):**
| Em7 B7 | Em7 A7 | Dm7 A7 | Dm7 | Dm7 A7 | Dm7 | Dm7 G7 | C6 |

## Level 1: General Concepts

### 1-1. The backdoor ii-V

**instruction:** How does the A section get back to C after going to F minor?

**response:** It uses a backdoor ii-V. From C6 (I), the harmony moves to chord iv made minor (Fm7), which becomes the ii of a ii-V: Fm7 – Bb7. In a regular ii-V you'd expect Bb7 to resolve down a fifth to E♭, but here it resolves *up a whole step* — through the back door — to C6 (the home tonic). The backdoor cadence (Bb7 is ♭VII7 of C) is a softer, hipper way to return home than a plain G7.

### 1-2. A 1-6-2-5-1 turned into a chain of secondary dominants

**instruction:** What drives the second half of the A section?

**response:** A 1-6-2-5-1 (C6 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7 – C6). To add momentum, every chord except the tonic is tweaked to a dominant 7, making a chain of V-I cadences down the cycle of fifths: A7 → D7 → G7 → C. A chromatic passing chord (Bb7, a half-step above A7) leads into the A7 in bar 3, which also creates a smooth descending bass line: C → B♭ → A.

### 1-3. Why chord I is a major 6, not a major 7

**instruction:** Why is the tonic written C6 instead of CMaj7?

**response:** Yardbird Suite is an early-1940s bebop-era tune, and in these older tunes the I chord is often a major 6 rather than a major 7. The major 7th already adds dissonance, and when the melody outlines the root (as it often does in older tunes), the root against the major 7th creates a harsh ♭9 interval. Using a major 6 chord avoids that double dissonance, which is the stylistically authentic sound here.

## Level 2: Song-Specific Analysis

### 2-1. The A section in full

**instruction:** Analyze the Yardbird Suite A section.

**response:** It opens on C6 (I), moves to the minor iv (Fm7) which becomes the ii of a backdoor ii-V (Fm7 – Bb7) resolving up a tone to C6. Then a 1-6-2-5-1 (C6 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7 – C6), with the vi, ii made dominant (A7, D7) for a cycle-of-fifths chain, and a chromatic Bb7 leading into A7 in bar 3. The first-time ending adds a V (G7) to relaunch; the second-time ending shuts down to lead into the bridge.

### 2-2. The bridge

**instruction:** Walk me through the Yardbird Suite bridge.

**response:** The bridge starts on chord iii (Em7) and re-cadences to it via a V-I (B7 → Em7). Then Em7 becomes the ii of a ii-V (Em7 – A7) resolving to Dm7 (chord ii, not D major). The next four bars repeat the idea from Dm7: a re-cadence (A7 → Dm7), then a ii-V-I (Dm7 – G7 – C6) into the final A. A common bass reharm walks chromatically down: Em7 → B7/D♯ → Em7/D → A7/C♯, and similarly through the Dm7 bars.

### 2-3. The melody: diatonic, with tension from the harmony

**instruction:** What makes the A-section melody sound "crunchy" while staying in key?

**response:** Most of the melody is crystal-clear C major, but it sounds interesting because of how it hangs on the shifting harmony. In bar 3 a single melodic idea is the 5th of C6, then the 13th of Bb7, then the 5th of A7. In bar 5, what is "3rd falling to root" (E → C) in C-major terms becomes the 9th and 7th over D7. It's a great compositional lesson: keep the melody diatonic and let the harmony underneath supply the tension. The melody only leaves the key at bar 2, bailing out onto the Fm7 (enclosing the 3rd, A♭) to acknowledge the backdoor cadence.

## Level 3: Performance Decisions

### 3-1. Soloing over the backdoor cadence

**instruction:** What do I play over Fm7 – Bb7 going to C?

**response:** Treat it as the minor-iv backdoor: over Fm7 think F Dorian (or A♭ major), and over Bb7 lean toward the notes that resolve up a step into C — the melody itself encloses the 3rd of Fm7 (A♭) before the line gets back to C major. Target a C chord tone (E or G) as the Bb7 resolves up. The whole gesture is brief, so spell it with clean target notes rather than a busy scale.

### 3-2. Bebop lines over the bridge

**instruction:** Why are F naturals played over the A7 in the bridge?

**response:** The bridge's home tonality at that point is D minor (the A7 is the V of Dm7). So the F♮ isn't a wrong note on A7 — it belongs to the prevailing D-minor tonality, and the bridge lines draw from D melodic minor across the A7–Dm7 area. Parker's melody here is textbook bebop: turns around the 3rd of Em7, chromatic approaches into chord tones, and enclosures. Think in the local key (D minor) rather than chord-by-chord and the "outside" notes make sense.

### 3-3. Comping the bass reharmonizations

**instruction:** How can I make the bridge's harmony more interesting when comping?

**response:** Use the chromatic descending-bass substitution: under Em7 – B7 – A7, drop the bass Em7 → B7/D♯ → Em7/D → A7/C♯ so the bass walks down by half steps while the chords stay essentially the same. The same trick works through the Dm7 bars (Dm7 → A7/C♯ → Dm7/C → G7/B). It would be overkill on every chorus, so use it for variety — and listen for the bass player outlining it.
