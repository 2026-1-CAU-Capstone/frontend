## Song Info

- **Title:** My Funny Valentine
- **Composer:** Richard Rodgers / Lorenz Hart (1937)
- **Center Key:** C minor
- **Form:** 36 bars (A A1 B C, with an extended ending)
- **Source:** The Jazz Pursuit — "My Funny Valentine Tutorial & Analysis" (https://youtu.be/O0YDA1z-ZWY)

### Diatonic Chords (C minor / relative major E♭)

Cm (i), Dm7♭5 (ii°), EbMaj7 (♭III / relative major), Fm7 (iv), Gm7 / G7 (v / V), AbMaj7 (♭VI), Bb7 (♭VII)

### Chord Progression

**A section (first bars):**
| Cm | CmMaj7 | Cm7 | Cm6 | AbMaj7 | Fm7 | Dm7♭5 G7♭9 | Cm |

(A static i chord with a descending inner line: C → B → B♭ → A. Then ♭VI, iv, and a minor ii-V-i home.)

**B section (modulates to relative major E♭):**
| ... | Fm7♭5 | Bb7♭9 | EbMaj7 | ... |

## Level 1: General Concepts

### 1-1. The descending line cliché over a static minor chord

**instruction:** What is the famous descending line in the My Funny Valentine A section?

**response:** The A section sits on chord i (C minor) but changes its quality each bar to walk a single voice downward: Cm → CmMaj7 → Cm7 → Cm6. The root stays C, while an inner voice descends chromatically C → B → B♭ → A. This "line cliché" (sometimes called the minor-major or "James Bond" line) creates motion and tension over an otherwise unchanging chord, and it's the harmonic signature of the tune's opening.

### 1-2. The minor ii-V-i

**instruction:** How does the A section resolve back home?

**response:** It uses a minor ii-V-i: Dm7♭5 – G7♭9 – Cm. In a minor key the ii chord is half-diminished (Dm7♭5) and the V is a dominant with a flat 9 (G7♭9), borrowing the leading tone (B♮) and ♭9 (A♭) from C harmonic minor. This gives a stronger, darker pull to the minor tonic than a plain v–i would, and it's the standard cadence for minor-key standards.

### 1-3. Moving to the relative major in the B section

**instruction:** Why does the tune brighten partway through?

**response:** The B section modulates to the relative major, E♭ (chord ♭III of C minor), which shares the same key signature as C minor. It gets there with its own ii-V-I: Fm7♭5 – Bb7♭9 – EbMaj7. Wait — note that in E♭ major a clean ii-V would be Fm7 – Bb7; the ♭5 and ♭9 colors here lean on the parent C-minor sound, easing the shift. Landing on EbMaj7 lifts the mood before the music settles back into C minor.

## Level 2: Song-Specific Analysis

### 2-1. The A section in full

**instruction:** Analyze the My Funny Valentine A section.

**response:** It opens on chord i (C minor) and holds that root while the chord quality shifts to drop an inner voice: Cm → CmMaj7 → Cm7 → Cm6 (the line C–B–B♭–A). From there it moves down to AbMaj7 (♭VI) and Fm7 (iv), then cadences home with a minor ii-V-i: Dm7♭5 – G7♭9 – Cm. So the section is a static-tonic line cliché followed by a descent to the subdominant region and a minor cadence back to i.

### 2-2. The form and its sections

**instruction:** What is the overall shape of the tune?

**response:** My Funny Valentine is 36 bars, longer than a standard 32-bar AABA. It runs as A, A1, B, C: two related A sections built on the C-minor line cliché, a B section that brightens to the relative major (E♭), and a closing C section that resolves the form. The relative-major detour in the middle is the main point of contrast against the brooding minor A sections, and the extended ending is what stretches the form past 32 bars.

## Level 3: Performance Decisions

### 3-1. Soloing over the line cliché

**instruction:** What do I play over the Cm → CmMaj7 → Cm7 → Cm6 opening?

**response:** Don't change scales every bar — the chord isn't really changing, only one voice is. Stay rooted in C minor and spell the descending line C → B → B♭ → A in your phrasing so the ear hears the cliché. C melodic minor covers Cm and CmMaj7 (it contains the B♮), then C natural minor / Dorian covers Cm7 and Cm6 (B♭ and A natural). The melodic goal is to voice that one descending note clearly rather than running busy lines.

### 3-2. Navigating the minor ii-V-i

**instruction:** What scales fit Dm7♭5 – G7♭9 – Cm?

**response:** Over Dm7♭5 use D Locrian (or D Locrian ♮2 for a smoother color); over G7♭9 use G altered or G half-whole diminished to get the ♭9 and the leading tone B♮; resolve to Cm with C natural or harmonic minor. The key target is landing on a strong C-minor chord tone (C, E♭, or G) right as the G7♭9 resolves — that arrival is the emotional anchor of the phrase.

### 3-3. Pacing the minor-to-major contrast

**instruction:** How should a solo reflect the shift to the relative major?

**response:** Treat the A sections as dark and introspective — lean into the line cliché and the minor cadence with space and restraint. When the B section lifts to EbMaj7 (relative major), open up: brighter, more lyrical lines, higher register, a sense of release. Then darken again as the music returns to C minor for the closing C section. Using that built-in major/minor contrast gives your chorus a natural emotional arc that matches the song's wistful character.
