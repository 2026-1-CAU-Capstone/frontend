## Song Info

- **Title:** Strasbourg / St. Denis
- **Composer:** Roy Hargrove (2008, from the album *Earfood*)
- **Center Key:** A♭ Major
- **Form:** 8-bar progression (looped for all sections — bassline intro, unison melody, call-and-response, solos)
- **Source:** The Jazz Pursuit — "Strasbourg / St. Denis (R. Hargrove) Lesson & Analysis" (https://youtu.be/I1DdIRSs2Bw)

### Diatonic Chords (A♭ Major)

AbMaj7 (I), Bbm7 (ii), Cm7 (iii), DbMaj7 (IV), Eb7 (V), Fm7 (vi), Gm7♭5 (vii°)

### Chord Progression

**8-bar form:**
| Bbm7 Cm7 | DbMaj7 F7♭9 | Bbm7 Cm7 | DbMaj7 F7♭9 | Bbm7 Cm7 | DbMaj7 Eb7 | AbMaj7 | F7♭9 |

(Walks up ii → iii → IV, cadences V-i back to ii — twice. The third time it keeps rising to V and finally resolves to I in the penultimate bar.)

---

## Level 1: General Concepts

### 1-1. A modern standard built on a single 8-bar loop

**instruction:** How is Strasbourg / St. Denis structured harmonically?

**response:** The whole tune — intro, melody, call-and-response, and solos — sits on one repeating 8-bar progression in A♭ major. Rather than a long through-composed form, everything is improvised and arranged over this short loop, which makes it a great introduction to playing within a groove. Learning the eight bars unlocks the entire piece.

### 1-2. Walking up the diatonic chords ii → iii → IV

**instruction:** What is the rising motion at the start of each two-bar cell?

**response:** Each cell climbs through the diatonic chords of A♭: Bbm7 (ii) → Cm7 (iii) → DbMaj7 (IV). This stepwise ascent through chords of the key is a simple, strong way to generate motion while staying entirely in A♭ major. It functions like a launch ramp that sets up the cadence that follows.

### 1-3. A V-I cadence onto a minor target (F7♭9 → Bbm7)

**instruction:** Why is the dominant written F7♭9, and where does it resolve?

**response:** The cadence target is Bbm7, treated as a local "one." Its dominant is F7, but because the chord of resolution is minor, you add a flat 9 — F7♭9 — borrowing the ♭9 (G♭) that pulls strongly into a minor chord. So F7♭9 → Bbm7 is a V-i into the ii chord, momentarily tonicizing Bbm7 before the loop restarts.

## Level 2: Song-Specific Analysis

### 2-1. The 8-bar progression in full

**instruction:** Walk me through the harmony.

**response:** Bars 1–2 walk up Bbm7 (ii) → Cm7 (iii) → DbMaj7 (IV), then cadence F7♭9 → back to Bbm7. Bars 3–4 repeat that exactly. Bars 5–6 begin the same climb (Bbm7 → Cm7 → DbMaj7), but instead of cadencing back, the harmony keeps rising to Eb7 (V). That V resolves to AbMaj7 (I) in bar 7 — the only appearance of the home chord. Bar 8 is one more F7♭9 to send the loop back to Bbm7.

### 2-2. The delayed tonic as a compositional event

**instruction:** Why does chord I appear so late in the form?

**response:** Most tunes state chord I early to plant the key, but here AbMaj7 doesn't arrive until the 7th bar of 8. By making the listener wait, Hargrove turns the tonic resolution into a real event rather than a given. For improvisers it's a built-in target: the whole loop leans toward that late-arriving AbMaj7, so aiming a phrase to land there gives your solo shape that matches the composition.

## Level 3: Performance Decisions

### 3-1. The melody and A♭ major pentatonic

**instruction:** What scale does the melody draw on, and how can I use it soloing?

**response:** The melody is almost entirely A♭ major pentatonic (A♭–B♭–C–E♭–F: root, 2, 3, 5, 6). Because the harmony only confirms the home key at the very end, the melody does the opposite — it stays firmly in A♭ throughout, keeping the tune grounded. Soloing, you can ride A♭ major pentatonic across the whole loop for a clean, vocal sound that works over every chord in the key.

### 3-2. Adding color beyond the pentatonic

**instruction:** How do I get past sounding "stuck" on the pentatonic?

**response:** The melody's final phrase breaks out of the pentatonic by using the full A♭ major scale and A♭ Mixolydian, with a bluesy G♭ at the end. That G♭ does double duty: it's the ♭7 of the key (a go-to blues note) and also the ♭9 of the F7 chord, so it reinforces the F7♭9 → Bbm7 cadence. Mixing in the full scale, Mixolydian, and that ♭7 blue note is the way to add color while staying connected to the changes.

### 3-3. Targeting the late tonic in a solo

**instruction:** How should I pace a solo over the looping form?

**response:** Treat the recurring F7♭9 → Bbm7 cadences as restless, unresolved points, and save your strongest melodic arrival for the AbMaj7 in bar 7 — the form's only true home. Build tension across the rising cells, then release onto that tonic. Because the form is short and repeats, develop a motive across several choruses and let the delayed-tonic structure give each chorus a natural climax.
