## Song Info

- **Title:** Satin Doll
- **Composer:** Duke Ellington / Billy Strayhorn (music), Johnny Mercer (lyrics) (1953)
- **Center Key:** C Major
- **Form:** 32 bars AABA (each section 8 bars)
- **Source:** The Jazz Pursuit — "Satin Doll (Ellington/Strayhorn) Lesson & Analysis" (https://youtu.be/lfcx9uswnZQ)

### Diatonic Chords (C Major)

CMaj7 (I), Dm7 (ii), Em7 (iii), FMaj7 (IV), G7 (V), Am7 (vi), Bm7♭5 (vii°)

### Chord Progression

**A section (8 bars):**
| Dm7 G7 | Dm7 G7 | Em7 A7 | Em7 A7 | Am7 D7 | Abm7 Db7 | CMaj7 | (turnaround) |

**Bridge (8 bars):**
| Gm7 C7 | Gm7 C7 | FMaj7 | FMaj7 | Am7 D7 | Am7 D7 | G7 | G7 |

## Level 1: General Concepts

### 1-1. A tune built from chained ii-V's

**instruction:** Satin Doll's A section is "packed with two-fives." What does that mean?

**response:** Almost every chord in the A section is the ii of a two-bar ii-V. Reading them: Dm7–G7 is a ii-V in C; Em7–A7 is a ii-V in D; Am7–D7 is a ii-V in G; and Abm7–Db7 is a ii-V that resolves down a semitone to C. So instead of one long progression, the A section is a series of short ii-V cells, most of which don't fully resolve before the next one begins. Recognizing each minor-7 chord as "the start of a two-five" is the key to memorizing and soloing on the tune.

### 1-2. The tritone-sub ii-V (Abm7 – Db7 → C)

**instruction:** Why does A♭m7 – D♭7 appear, and how does it resolve to C?

**response:** D♭7 is the tritone substitute of G7 (the normal V of C); both share the same guide tones (the 3rd and 7th, B/F and F/C♭ enharmonically). Preceding it with its own ii, A♭m7, makes a complete ii-V — but a substitute one — that resolves by descending a semitone, D♭7 → CMaj7, instead of by the usual fourth. The half-step root drop gives a smoother, hipper approach to the tonic than a plain G7, which is exactly the Ellington/Strayhorn flavor.

## Level 2: Song-Specific Analysis

### 2-1. The A section as four ii-V cells

**instruction:** Analyze the Satin Doll A section.

**response:** The A section is four two-bar ii-V cells. Dm7–G7 (ii-V in C, repeated) sets the home key but doesn't resolve. It steps up to Em7–A7 (ii-V in D). Then Am7–D7 (ii-V in G). Finally Abm7–Db7, a tritone-sub ii-V, resolves down a semitone to CMaj7 (chord I) — the first real arrival. So the section delays resolution through a string of unresolved ii-V's and only lands on the tonic at the end, then a turnaround sends it back around.

### 2-2. The bridge: modulating to IV, then back via ii-V

**instruction:** Analyze the Satin Doll bridge.

**response:** The bridge briefly tonicizes F major: Gm7–C7 is a ii-V in F, resolving to FMaj7 (held two bars). It then sets up the return to the home key with Am7–D7 (a ii-V in G) and G7, the home dominant, which pulls back to the final A section. So the bridge is essentially "ii-V to IV, sit on IV, then ii-V back to the home dominant" — a clean contrast to the A section's restless chain of cells.

## Level 3: Performance Decisions

### 3-1. Soloing through the ii-V chain

**instruction:** How do I improvise over the A section's stack of two-fives?

**response:** Play each ii-V in its own key and connect them with guide tones (3rds and 7ths). Over Dm7–G7 think C major; over Em7–A7 think D major; over Am7–D7 think G major; over Abm7–Db7 use the tritone-sub sound (D♭ Lydian-dominant / A♭ as its ii, or simply G altered since D♭7 ≈ G7alt). Because the cells move quickly (two beats each at the start), targeting the 3rd of each chord on the strong beat keeps your line spelling the changes without needing to think full scales.

### 3-2. The tritone-sub approach to the tonic

**instruction:** What scale fits Abm7 – Db7 going to C?

**response:** Treat D♭7 as a Lydian-dominant chord (D♭ Mixolydian with a ♯11) — that's the bright tritone-sub color resolving down to CMaj7. Equivalently, you can think G altered, since D♭7 and G7alt share guide tones and resolve to C. Over the A♭m7 just before it, A♭ Dorian works. The melodic goal is a smooth half-step descent into a C chord tone (e.g., landing on E or G of C) right as D♭7 resolves.

### 3-3. Contrasting the A section and bridge

**instruction:** How should my solo reflect the difference between the A section and the bridge?

**response:** The A section is restless — short, unresolved ii-V cells climbing through keys — so busier, change-spelling lines suit it. The bridge settles into clear key areas (F major, then back to the home dominant), so it invites longer, more singing phrases that relax the tension before the last A. Using that structural contrast — agitation in the A's, release in the bridge — gives your chorus a built-in narrative shape that matches the tune's design.
